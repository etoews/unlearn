Unlearning in the AI era

This section shows why the workarounds we wrote for older models are so hard to let go.

Citations: none.

Learn, unlearn, relearn

"The illiterate of the 21st century will not be those who cannot read and write, but those who cannot learn, unlearn, and relearn."

"By instructing students how to learn, unlearn and relearn, a powerful new dimension can be added to education."
Alvin Toffler, Future Shock, 1970

"Tomorrow's illiterate will not be the man who can't read; he will be the man who has not learned how to learn."
Herbert Gerjuoy, quoted in Future Shock

The famous quote is struck out because it merges two passages, so even the unlearning quote needed unlearning.

Toffler's real line says teaching students to learn, unlearn and relearn adds a new dimension to education.

The "illiterate" half belongs to Herbert Gerjuoy, whom Toffler quotes in the next paragraph.

The same chapter says to learn "how and when" to replace old ideas, and when is what this talk is about.

Citations:

  • "By instructing students how to learn, unlearn and relearn": Toffler, A., Future Shock, 1970, ch. 18. The archive.org OCR text reads "leam" for "learn", so search it for "unlearn and relearn". archive.org · full text
  • "Tomorrow's illiterate will not be the man who can't read": Future Shock, ch. 18, next paragraph ("Psychologist Herbert Gerjuoy of the Human Resources Research Organization phrases it simply"; the OCR text reads "Tomorrow's illiterate will not be the man who can't read; he will be the man who has not learned how to leam.").
  • "how and when" (spoken): Future Shock, ch. 18 ("Students must learn how to discard old ideas, how and when to replace them.").
  • Struck-through quote is a misattribution: wist.info ("This ubiquitous (mis)quotation of Toffler is a conflation of two sentences in ch. 18 of Toffler's Future Shock (1970).") wist.info · kottke.org ("It turns out that’s not a direct quote — it’s been cobbled together from two separate passages in the book") kottke.org

Where scaffolding lives

  • Your fingers
  • Your memory files
  • Your system prompts

Habits in your fingers outlive the rules that created them.

Memory files keep old rules alive, like my em-dash ban that survives in copies.

System prompts carry old scaffolding too: Anthropic cut over 80% of Claude Code's, but only after its evals showed no loss.

Citations:

  • "Your memory files": my own memory and prompt files still hold an em-dash ban removed from CLAUDE.md in Aug 2026.
  • "Your system prompts", "over 80%" (spoken): Shihipar, T., "The new rules of context engineering for Claude 5 generation models", Anthropic, 24 Jul 2026 ("We removed over 80% of Claude Code’s system prompt for models like Claude Opus 5 and Claude Fable 5 with no measurable loss on our coding evaluations."). claude.dev

Every generation: delete this

  • "CRITICAL: You MUST use this tool when..."
    → "Use this tool when..."
  • "Verify your work"
  • "No bullet points"
  • "Think carefully"

Each line was right for an older model, and each new prompting guide says to delete it.

"CRITICAL: You MUST use this tool when..." makes newer, more responsive models overtrigger, so soften it to "Use this tool when...".

"Verify your work" now causes over-verification and unnecessary testing, and Anthropic and OpenAI both say so.

"No bullet points" now pushes the model the wrong way.

Removing "Think carefully" made Opus 5.5 reply sooner with no clear loss in quality.

Citations:

  • "CRITICAL: You MUST use this tool when..." → "Use this tool when...": "Prompting best practices", Claude Platform Docs ("Claude Opus 4.5 and Claude Opus 4.6 are also more responsive to the system prompt than previous models.   • "Verify your work": "Prompting Claude Opus 5", Claude Platform Docs ("Claude Opus 5 verifies its own work without being told to." and "instructions like these cause over-verification on Claude Opus 5, and removing them reduces wasted tokens with no loss in quality"). platform.claude.com · "Prompting best practices" ("On Claude Opus 5, remove these instructions rather than rewriting them."). platform.claude.com

Why you can't see it

  • Habit fires first
  • 3 prompts feel like proof
  • Losses loom larger

System 1 fires first and applies old patterns to a new model.

A three-prompt vibe check passes a prompt that fails one time in four 42% of the time.

Deleting a rule risks a visible loss while keeping it only hides a gain, so we keep it.

The rule you worked hardest to perfect is the hardest to drop.

Citations:

  • "Habit fires first": "Thinking, Fast and Slow", Wikipedia, revision 8 Sep 2026 ("associating new information with existing patterns"). wikipedia.org
  • "3 prompts feel like proof": same Wikipedia revision (WYSIATI, "a small and necessarily un-representative set of observations"); 42% from Anthropic, "Demystifying evals for AI agents", 9 Jan 2026 ("If your agent has a 75% per-trial success rate and you run 3 trials, the probability of passing all three is (0.75)³ ≈ 42%."). anthropic.com
  • "Losses loom larger": same Wikipedia revision ("more likely to act to avert a loss than to achieve a gain").
  • "Hardest to drop" (spoken): Sutton, R., "The Bitter Lesson", 13 Mar 2019 ("personally satisfying to the researcher"). incompleteideas.net

Evals turn weeks into days

  • Weeks → days
  • Anthropic: evals first
  • You: vibes

Teams with evals upgrade to a new model in days, while teams without spend weeks testing.

Anthropic ran its evals before it cut 80% of its system prompt.

Users are told to test changes by observing behaviour, which is vibes.

Anatomy of an eval

Each case pairs an input with a ground truth answer.

The config is the model, effort and system prompt under test.

The config produces one output per case, and the config plus its outputs is one experiment.

Judges score each output, with one judge per metric.

Results hold each score, the judge's reason and the cost, using the STE rule as the running example.

Citations:

  • "Cases… ground truth", "Judges": Husain, H. & Shankar, S., "AI Evals: Everything You Need to Know", 18 Sep 2026. hamel.dev
  • "Config", "One experiment", "scores · why · cost": the ai-evals skill. github.com

Judges have biases

  • Give it the answer
  • Long ≠ good
  • Pin it. Binary. Read why.

Giving the judge a reference answer cut false passes on wrong maths answers from 14 in 20 to 3 in 20.

Judges favour longer answers, which matters with a model as verbose as Opus 5.

Pin the judge model, use pass/fail labels and read the judge's explanations.

Citations:

  • "Give it the answer": Zheng, L. et al., "Judging LLM-as-a-Judge with MT-Bench and Chatbot Arena", 2023, Table 4 ("Judge failure rate on 10 math questions with different prompts." and "A failure means when GPT-4 says an incorrect answer is correct."; the Default, CoT and Reference columns read 14/20, 6/20 and 3/20). arXiv HTML · arXiv
  • "Long ≠ good": Zheng et al. 2023 ("Verbosity bias is when an LLM judge favors longer, verbose responses, even if they are not as clear, high-quality, or accurate as shorter alternatives."). arXiv HTML · paddo.dev, "A Dial Worth Turning" ("510 words per response for Opus 5, against 158 for Opus 4.5"). paddo.dev
  • "Binary": Husain & Shankar ("Binary evaluations force clearer thinking and more consistent labeling"). hamel.dev

Read results honestly

  • Run it again
  • Cost beside score
  • Evals expire too

Run each case several times, because one run is noise.

Read cost beside score, because cheaper and buggier is still a regression.

An eval lasts only one to three model generations before it needs unlearning too.

Citations:

  • "Run it again": Anthropic, "Demystifying evals for AI agents" ("pass^k measures the probability that all k trials succeed. As k increases, pass^k falls since demanding consistency across more trials is a harder bar to clear."). anthropic.com · Arize Phoenix, "Repetitions". arize.com
  • "Evals expire too": Cherny, B., "Boris Cherny: Building Claude Code", Root Access transcript of Y Combinator Startup School, 27 Jul 2026 ("An eval might live for maybe one, two, three model generations."). transcript · YouTube · Martin, L., "Automating eval design and hillclimbing with Claude", 28 Sep 2026 ("If you pick cases because today's model fails them, you are sampling the valleys of one model's capability surface"). claude.dev

The unlearning experiment

The same cases run through all three arms.

The baseline is the old model with your scaffolding.

The candidate is the new model with your scaffolding.

The ablation is the new model with the scaffolding removed, which is an eval where you delete things.

Comparing the arms tells you whether to keep the scaffolding, unlearn it or add something new.

Citations:

  • "Ablation": Cherny, B., "Boris Cherny: Building Claude Code", Root Access transcript of Y Combinator Startup School, 27 Jul 2026 ("The ablation is essentially an eval, but you delete things to figure out the impact."). transcript · YouTube

Verdict

Keep · Unlearn · Rewrite

  • STE style: 6/9 vs 2/9
  • Facts: no change
  • Cost: 2.4× with STE
  • Length: ~480 words, both

The verdict is neither keep nor unlearn: rewrite the rule for its real goal, then test that.

STE style passed 6 of 9 runs with the rule and 2 of 9 without, so the line still changes Opus 5.5's style.

Fact scores didn't change, so the rule adds nothing to accuracy.

Runs with STE cost 2.4 times as much, because the rule doubles the thinking.

Both arms averaged about 480 words, 3.5 times the ground truth, so the rule never fixed length.

Citations:

  • "6/9 vs 2/9", "Facts: no change", "2.4×", "~480 words": my own runs of evals/ste, 26 Oct 2026. Scores are passes out of 9 runs per arm (3 cases × 3 repetitions), judged by Fable 5.1. Task cost $1.13 vs $0.47 in Phoenix. Ground truth averages 142 words. github.com/etoews/unlearn

Start unlearning

This section turns the method into a first step you can take on Monday.

Citations: none.

New model, new workarounds

  • "thoughts? = discuss"
  • Written for Opus 5
  • Give it an expiry

I'm still writing workarounds, like a rule that "thoughts?" means discuss rather than start work.

I wrote it for Opus 5, so it needs the same test when the next model ships.

Give every rule an expiry condition and record the incident it exists for.

Citations:

  • "thoughts? = discuss", "Written for Opus 5": my own CLAUDE.md commit, 19 Sep 2026 ("because the default reading is to start work").
  • "Give it an expiry": Zaninotto, F., "The State Of AI Harness Engineering 2026", Marmelab, 24 Sep 2026 ("give every control an expiry condition, and write down the incident it exists for"). marmelab.com

Start with a linter

  • /doctor prompt-audit
  • Shows what looks stale
  • Mine: "nothing stale"
  • Then: test it

The /doctor prompt-audit command checks your instruction files for lines written for older models.

It only shows what looks stale, so it's a linter, not a measurement.

It found nothing stale in my CLAUDE.md three weeks after I added an Opus 5 rule.

After the linter, test the remaining lines with an eval.

Citations:

  • "/doctor prompt-audit", "Shows what looks stale": "Memory", Claude Code Docs, section "Audit your instruction files" ("run /doctor prompt-audit in a session" and "instructions written for older models"). code.claude.com
  • "Mine: nothing stale": my own /doctor prompt-audit run, 11 Oct 2026 ("no workarounds for older models").

Monday

  • One line
  • 20 real cases
  • With vs without

Pick the one line in your prompt you can't justify.

Build 20 cases from real failures.

Run an eval with the line and without it, and let the results decide.
